The Constitution isn’t finished.

What should come next?

More than 190 countries have written constitutions. Together they contain thousands of ideas about rights, government, democracy, equality, and public life.

What can Americans learn from them?

The project uses Constitute, a global database of the world’s constitutions, as a source of ideas.

How it works

Explore. Choose. Draft.

This is not a quiz about constitutional law. It is an invitation to look beyond familiar choices and decide what belongs in America’s constitutional future.

01

The research

What happens when Americans look outward?

The project studies constitutional preferences, borrowing, and the effects of exposing citizens to constitutional alternatives beyond the United States.

What do people want?

Which rights and reforms rise to the top? Which divide people, and which attract surprisingly broad agreement?

Where do ideas come from?

Which constitutions, countries, and regions become sources of inspiration for Americans thinking about constitutional change?

How do ideas travel?

How does a provision written elsewhere change when an American participant translates it into a proposed U.S. amendment?

Does exploration change attitudes?

Does encountering unfamiliar constitutional possibilities affect confidence in the U.S. Constitution—or openness to changing it?

Pilot principle: keep the first study focused. The initial instrument will prioritize the amendment selected, its source, the participant’s proposed text and rationale, and a short set of pre/post constitutional attitudes.

About

A constitutional drafting lab for the public.

The 28th Amendment Project grows out of research on public constitution making and earlier drafting exercises conducted with the National Constitution Center. It asks a simple question with a surprisingly global answer: when Americans are invited to imagine constitutional change, what do they choose?
